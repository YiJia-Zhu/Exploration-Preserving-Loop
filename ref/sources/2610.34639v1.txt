Abstract
How do later loops in a pretrained looped language model repair two-hop retrieval? We study Ouro-1.4B
and Ouro-2.6B by transplanting attention queries between loops at the same layer, head and final prompt
position. On synthetic in-context questions, injecting the layer-indexed collection of loop-3 queries
throughout loop 1 rescues 90% and 97% of questions answered incorrectly at loop 1 and correctly at
loop 3, versus 24% and 21% for shuffled donors. The rescue also holds under greedy generation in
Ouro-2.6B; in Ouro-1.4B it is limited to four-candidate ranking. Crossed donor–recipient swaps favor
retrieval through the donor’s bridge entity over copying its answer, although a substantial positional
component remains in Ouro-2.6B. Late keys and values rescue far fewer targets. Freezing queries at their
loop-1 values also damages two-hop accuracy more than a matched question that supplies the bridge.
On natural questions, rescue is smaller and replicates only in Ouro-2.6B, and crossed swaps do not
establish bridge redirection. These results identify a transferable collection of late-loop queries
that supports bridge-conditioned retrieval on synthetic scenes; they do not locate where the bridge
is computed or establish a single-vector handoff.

1 Introduction
Language models often fail to compose two facts they know separately. On two-hop questions about facts
stored in the weights, if the bridge entity is resolved late, the remaining layers may no longer
carry out the second hop (Biran et al., 2024), and evidence for latent use of the second hop is only
moderate (Yang et al., 2024). Patching a later-layer state back into an earlier layer repairs some of
these failures, but only if the best layer pair is chosen for each item (Biran et al., 2024). In
models trained from scratch, reusing layers on a second pass improves out-of-distribution two-hop
generalization (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026).
Looped language models build this reuse into the architecture of pretrained models.
Ouro (Zhu and others, 2025) applies its layer stack four times to every token and reads out a prediction
after each loop; its authors conjecture that this recurrent structure is why small looped models
manipulate synthetic knowledge better than non-looped models of the same size (Zhu and others, 2025, Sec. 6).
Pretrained looped models have mostly been analyzed through per-loop answers (Olszowiec et al., 2026),
patches between runs at the same loop (Wu et al., 2026; Anonymous, 2026a), or interventions on
whole loops (Anonymous, 2026b; Wang and Reid, 2026), rather than by moving a later loop’s query into
an earlier loop.
Attention offers a natural way to split the problem: at the answer position, the query determines
what to look for; at the fact positions, the keys determine where each fact is found and the
values what it contributes. In a looped model, each layer and head applies the same weights in
every loop, so a query computed in loop 3 can be placed into loop 1 at exactly the same layer, head and
position, with no search over layer pairs. Our intervention supplies each layer with its own late-loop
query; it does not insert a single vector once. Sequential query mechanisms have been identified in
small models trained on two-hop tasks (Guo et al., 2025). Here we test whether queries in pretrained
looped models can be transferred across recurrent passes at matching coordinates, and what retrieval
behavior that transfer induces. We study Ouro-1.4B and Ouro-2.6B on synthetic in-context
two-hop questions (“What color is in Bob’s box?”), each paired with matched one-hop questions. In both
models, loop 1 answers nearly every one-hop question but fails on 42% (1.4B) and 35% (2.6B) of the
two-hop ones.
Our contributions are as follows:
• 
A cross-loop query handoff. In a setting fixed before the first run, placing the late
queries at the answer position into loop 1, with keys and values at earlier prompt positions
left at loop 1, corrects
most of the loop-1 failures that loop 3 corrects, far more than another example’s query does
(Section 4); holding the query at its loop-1 value removes the loop gain
(Section 6).
• 
What the handoff supports. Crossed swaps favor retrieval through the donor’s bridge
over copying its answer, with a substantial positional component in Ouro-2.6B. Late keys and
values recover far less (Section 5).
• 
A matched control. Against a question that names the bridge, the query freeze is
composition-selective in both models, and more so than freezing the values of the fact line
both questions read (Section 6).
• 
A boundary on natural text. On natural two-hop questions, the Ouro-2.6B rescue
replicates on new items but is smaller; Ouro-1.4B shows no rescue, and a crossed swap shows no
redirection (Section 7).
Together, these results identify a collection of late-loop queries at the answer position that can be
transferred to an earlier loop and favors bridge-conditioned retrieval on synthetic scenes. Under this
intervention, keys and values at earlier prompt positions remain at their loop-1 states, although
freezing experiments also show that native loop gains depend on updates to fact values
(Section 6). We use handoff for this transfer across matching layer and head
coordinates; the experiments neither establish a single-vector bottleneck nor locate where the bridge
is computed.
Our scope is limited. All results are on Ouro, and most are on synthetic scenes. The handoff weakens
beyond the base template. On natural questions, it is smaller in Ouro-2.6B and absent in Ouro-1.4B, which
also fails our behavioral gate on two of seven other templates (Sections 7
and 8); the boundary thus depends on model size and format. Ouro-1.4B’s loop 1 generates
no answer, so its rescue shows only in a ranking among candidates, and our two dense controls do not
isolate looping as the source of the rescue (Section 4).

2 Setup
Ouro.
Ouro (Zhu and others, 2025) is a family of looped decoder-only transformers. A stack of
LL layers (L=24L=24 in Ouro-1.4B and L=48L=48 in Ouro-2.6B; 16 attention heads per layer with 16 key–value
heads, head dimension 128, rotary position embeddings) is applied T=4T=4 times. In loop tt, the whole
sequence passes through the stack, and head hh of layer ℓ\ell attends using the queries, keys and values
computed in that loop and layer from the normalized attention input xi(t,ℓ)x^{(t,\ell)}_{i} at position ii:

Because the weights are shared across loops, a coordinate (ℓ,h,i)(\ell,h,i) refers to the same weights in
every loop, and all differences between loops come from the inputs. The output of loop tt is the input
of loop t+1t+1. A shared final norm and language-model head produce a next-token distribution after every
loop, which we call the loop-tt exit. Ouro trains all exits jointly: the pretraining loss weights
the next-token loss at each exit by a learned exit distribution, with an entropy term that corresponds to
a uniform prior over exits (Zhu and others, 2025, Sec. 3.3). Ouro-2.6B was obtained from the 24-layer model
by duplicating its layers after the first 3 trillion training tokens (Zhu and others, 2025, Sec. 4.3), so the
two sizes are not independent replications. We read the loop-tt exit directly and do not use the
learned exit gate.
Task.
Each scene has four people, four boxes (A–D) and four colors, linked by random
bijections and written as eight fact lines (“Box D contains blue.”, “Bob has box D.”). All box–color
lines precede all person–box lines, and the lines are shuffled within each block
(Appendix A). The two-hop question asks for the target entity (a color) from the
source entity (a person) through the bridge entity (a box): “Question: What color is in
Bob’s box?” followed by “Answer:”. The matched one-hop questions in the same scene ask for the bridge
from the source (person→\tobox) and for the target from the bridge (box→\tocolor). We score the four
candidates of the question’s type (all single tokens) by their next-token log-probability at a loop exit;
chance is 25%. Table 1 lists the notation. Let aj,i​(t)∈{0,1}a_{j,i}(t)\in\{0,1\} indicate
whether question type j∈{2,1​a,1​b}j\in\{2,1a,1b\} is answered correctly in scene ii at loop tt, and let

Interventions.
Each intervention replaces one attention component at a fixed coordinate with
its value from another loop or another prompt and lets the rest of the forward pass run as usual; queries
and keys are replaced after RoPE.
Freezes (dependence on native updates) hold one component at its own loop-1 value in loops 2–4, in every layer and
head: the query at qq (IQI_{Q}), or the keys (IKI_{K}) or values (IVI_{V}) at every position in ℱ\mathcal{F}.
Matched-size controls freeze the same component outside the question and outside ℱ\mathcal{F}: the keys or values
of the two unused fact lines (CKC_{K}, CVC_{V}), or the query at one other position (CQC_{Q}). The
retained loop gain is ρI=[A2I​(3)−A2​(1)]/[A2​(3)−A2​(1)]\rho_{I}=[A^{I}_{2}(3)-A_{2}(1)]/[A_{2}(3)-A_{2}(1)] (loop 1 is never
modified), and the selective causal damage SCD⁡(I)\mathrm{SCD}(I) is the two-hop accuracy loss at
loop 3 minus the larger of the two one-hop losses (the one-hop loss). ρ\rho measures how much of
the loop gain survives a freeze, and SCD how selectively the freeze hurts composition; we report both
because they can disagree.
The rescue (sufficiency under query transplantation) replaces the query at qq, in loop rr and in every layer and head, with
the same example’s query from a later loop ss (the late query):
Qℓ​h,q(r)←Qℓ​h,q(s)Q^{(r)}_{\ell h,q}\leftarrow Q^{(s)}_{\ell h,q} for every (ℓ,h)(\ell,h).
The donor queries come from an unmodified forward pass. The recipient receives a separate cached query
at each layer and head; its residual stream and subsequent computations otherwise evolve under the
intervention. Since qq is the last prompt position and attention is causal, every query, key and value
at positions before qq keeps its native loop-rr value. Keys and values at qq itself are not patched
and may change in later layers as a consequence of earlier query patches. Thus, “late query” denotes
a layer-indexed collection, not a single state inserted once. Appendix B gives the
intervention sequence. Targets are two-hop
questions answered wrongly at loop rr and correctly at loop ss. The rescue rate is the share of targets
answered correctly at the loop-rr exit after the patch, and Δrescue\Delta_{\mathrm{rescue}} is this rate minus the rate for a
shuffled donor (another target’s loop-ss query). Synthetic rescue uses donors with the same prompt
token length; natural-QA donor selection and rotary position alignment are specified in
Section 7. The primary setting
(r=1r=1, s=3s=3, the final position qq, all layers and heads) was fixed before the first rescue round and
never changed; other loops and layer ranges serve as robustness checks. Rescue rates are conditional
on this target set, not accuracies over all questions or net improvements on the full set. They do not
measure whether patching would damage questions already answered correctly at loop rr.
Swaps place a component from a donor prompt into a recipient prompt. Unless stated otherwise, we
read a patched model at the exit of the patched loop, and we state the donor rule for each
result (Anonymous, 2026a).
Protocol.
Each experiment had a design specifying its inputs, intervention sites, metrics,
primary tests and stopping rules, committed before that experiment was run. Follow-up experiments could
therefore be designed after earlier results were available; analyses described as post hoc were not
pre-specified primary tests. On synthetic
scenes, identity patches reproduce the unpatched outputs bitwise, and an independent script recomputed
every primary statistic. Each experiment uses newly sampled scenes: 400 per model for the freezes (each
with one two-hop and two one-hop questions) and, unless stated otherwise, 600 elsewhere. Intervals are
95% paired bootstrap percentile intervals over scenes (over items for natural QA; 20,000 resamples),
Bonferroni-corrected within each experiment’s primary tests where stated (“Bonferroni kk” marks a
correction over kk tests). Differences between rates are given in percentage points (points).
3 In Ouro, the loop gain is specific to two-hop composition
Before analyzing a model’s mechanism, we require its loop gain to be specific to composition (the
behavioral gate): a two-hop gain on 𝒮⋆\mathcal{S}^{\star} whose 95% interval lies above zero, and
Gcomp>0G_{\mathrm{comp}}>0. On the 400 freeze scenes, two-hop accuracy rises from 58.3% at loop 1 to
93.8% at loop 3 in Ouro-1.4B and from 65.3% to 99.5% in Ouro-2.6B, gains of 35.5 points
[30.5,40.5][30.5,40.5] and 34.2 points [29.5,39.0][29.5,39.0]. The one-hop questions are near ceiling from loop 1 onward
(95.5–99.5%), with mean gains of at most 3.5 and 0.75 points (Table 7). On 𝒮⋆\mathcal{S}^{\star}
(n=364n=364 and 393), the two-hop gain is 33.8 [28.9,39.0][28.9,39.0] and 33.3 [28.8,38.2][28.8,38.2] points, and on the
full set GcompG_{\mathrm{comp}} is 28.7 [23.5,34.0][23.5,34.0] and 33.0 [28.5,37.8][28.5,37.8]: the loop gain comes mostly
from composition rather than from better one-hop lookup. Both Ouro models therefore pass the gate.

Looping versus depth.
Unlike back-patching (Biran et al., 2024), where the layer pair is
chosen for each item, one intervention specification fixed in advance repairs most targets here. In two dense controls, a
late query from deeper layers barely rescues: 1.7 points [−2.5,5.9][-2.5,5.9] in
LayerSkip-Llama3-8B (Elhoushi et al., 2024), which is trained to answer at intermediate layers (a
query from 16 layers deeper, patched into every layer below exit 16), and at most 8.9 [3.9,15.1][3.9,15.1] in
Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Yang and others, 2025) (patched at one layer; the interval extends above the 10-point bound
we set in advance). Neither control isolates looping: the dense depth offsets are at most 16 layers,
against 48 in Ouro-1.4B.
5 Crossed query swaps favor bridge-conditioned retrieval
If the late query carried the answer, the model should output the donor’s color; if the resolved
bridge (“look up box X”), the recipient’s color for the donor’s box; if a position, the recipient’s
color at the line position of the donor’s answer. Following the crossed design
that Kale et al. (2026) apply to the residual stream of non-looped models, donor and recipient ask
about the same person, who holds a different box in each, so these three predictions and the
recipient’s own answer are four different colors on every item.

Patched into loop 1, the donor’s late query most often makes the model return the recipient’s color for
the donor’s bridge box (Table 3): 45.0% and 48.0%, against 13.3% and 8.2% for the
donor’s answer. The bridge-consistent outcome is the largest category, supporting bridge-conditioned

A matched control.
The one-hop questions differ from the two-hop question in length and
wording. A pre-specified control (400 new scenes per model) therefore pairs each two-hop question TT
with a question GG that names the bridge (“What color is in box D which Bob has?”) and has the same
length, answer, candidates and fact lines. This controls these features while supplying the bridge
explicitly; the question wording also changes. We
define MM as TT’s accuracy loss at loop 3 minus GG’s. Holding the query at qq at its loop-1 value
costs TT 38.2 and 35.0 points and GG 21.2 and 4.8 points, so M=17.0M=17.0 [11.5,22.8][11.5,22.8] and 30.330.3
[25.0,35.5][25.0,35.5] (Bonferroni 2): the query state is composition-selective in both models, although in
Ouro-1.4B it also partly serves generic retrieval. In Ouro-2.6B, GG is near ceiling from loop 1 onward
(96.5%), so there MM mostly reflects the fact that only TT has a loop gain.
A second pre-specified run, on new scenes, freezes each used line separately. For the box→\tocolor line,
which both questions read, freezing the values costs TT and GG about equally (M=−1.2M=-1.2 and 4.84.8) and
keeps most of the gain (ρ=0.69\rho=0.69 and 0.640.64); this freeze is less selective than the query freeze by
14.3 [8.8,19.8][8.8,19.8] and 22.5 [16.8,28.3][16.8,28.3] points (Bonferroni 2). Freezing the values of the
person→\tobox line, which only TT reads, removes most or all of TT’s gain (ρ=0.26\rho=0.26 and
−0.85-0.85); this design cannot tell whether that reflects composition or only the fact that GG does not
read the line. On matched material, the clearest composition signal thus comes from the query, while
later loops also rewrite part of the memory.
Under these freezes, preserving the native loop gain depends on updates to both queries and fact
values, with interactions between keys and values. Conversely, the query rescue tests whether late
queries can recover answers while the memory at earlier prompt positions remains at loop 1.

No redirection in a crossed swap.
To repeat the crossed swap of Section 5 on
natural text, we add one passage to the recipient’s prompt: the donor’s second-hop passage, with its
answer replaced by another answer of the same type. Following the donor’s bridge, copying the donor’s
answer and giving the recipient’s own answer then correspond to different candidates. In a pre-specified
test on the 711 pairs in which Ouro-2.6B answers both items correctly at loop 3, the donor’s late query
makes the model follow the donor’s bridge on 27.7% of pairs, and an unrelated item’s late query on
26.2% (+1.5+1.5 [−0.1,3.2][-0.1,3.2], Bonferroni 2; not supported); patching only the last quarter of the layers
gives the same result (+1.8+1.8 [0.0,3.7][0.0,3.7]). Without any patch, the edited passage alone draws this
choice on 25.7% of pairs, and the donor’s answer is almost never chosen (at most 7% in any arm). On
these items, we therefore cannot establish that the late query carries the resolved intermediate.
The positive rescue result alone does not establish the synthetic bridge-retrieval mechanism on natural
text. Nor are freezes on natural items
composition-selective against a bridge-given question (SCD within 2 points of zero).
8 The rescue weakens beyond the base template
Templates and formats.
On three template variants (V1 shuffles the facts and adds distractor
lines; V2 rewords the names, containers, attribute, relation and question; V3 does both), Ouro-2.6B passes
the gate and the rescue still works, though more weakly (rescue 44.0–76.5% of targets against
96.9%); Ouro-1.4B fails the gate on V2 and V3 (Gcomp=−6.0G_{\mathrm{comp}}=-6.0 and −8.8-8.8). On four formats
with shuffled facts (lockers, rooms, cars and a short story), gate and rescue pass in all eight cells,
again more weakly: the late query rescues 60–79% of targets in Ouro-2.6B
and 52–71% in Ouro-1.4B, where a shuffled donor gets 16–45%.
Larger scenes.
With 8 people, boxes and colors, distractor lines and eight-way scoring, both
models pass the gate and the rescue holds (Δrescue\Delta_{\mathrm{rescue}} 30.5 and 35.5 points; rescue 38–48% of targets,
shuffled donor 7.0–12.9%). With 16 of each, Ouro-2.6B still passes (Δrescue=26.3\Delta_{\mathrm{rescue}}=26.3),
and Ouro-1.4B fails the gate. On the four formats with 8 and 16 entities, Ouro-2.6B passes the gate in all
eight cells and Ouro-1.4B in six: it fails the gate on the locker and story formats at 16 entities, which
leaves the rescue untested there. Wherever the gate passes, the late query beats a shuffled donor’s by
16–35 points in Ouro-2.6B and by 18–29 in Ouro-1.4B, while the rescue rate falls from 31–48% of
targets at 8 entities to 20–25% at 16 (kk-way scoring).
9 Related work
Latent multi-hop reasoning and patching.
Letting lower layers attend to higher layers
improves multi-hop answers (Yu et al., 2025), and Patchscopes repair two-hop failures by patching a
first-hop representation into a second-hop prompt (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024). In a three-layer
toy model of in-context two-hop reasoning, a later layer’s query built from the copied bridge retrieves the
target (Guo et al., 2025), providing a precedent for the sequential query mechanism (see also
Ye et al., 2025). Our contribution is to test transfer across loops at matching coordinates in
pretrained models and to distinguish bridge-consistent retrieval from answer copying. Query patching
itself is an established tool: filter-head queries carry a predicate (Sen Sharma et al., 2026); our
crossed swaps instead test entity-conditioned retrieval. Bound entities are retrieved by
pointer and positional mechanisms (Prakash et al., 2025; Gur-Arieh et al., 2025; Zhou, 2026), and
routing heads carry multilingual two-hop reasoning in dense LMs (Kim et al., 2026b).
Looped language models.
Weight-shared depth goes back to the Universal
Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2019), and looping has been argued to suit compositional
reasoning (Saunshi et al., 2025; Popescu et al., 2026). Interpretability work on looped LMs finds
fixed points with stable head behavior and heads that keep their roles across
iterations (Blayney et al., 2026; Olszowiec et al., 2026), limited evidence of decodable
intermediate results in Huginn (Lu et al., 2025), cross-loop structure in Ouro and
Huginn (Wang and Reid, 2026), and, in Ouro-1.4B, an intermediate of two-step arithmetic that a probe
reads but the model does not use (Shalev Merin, 2026). In models trained from scratch,
bridge entities become causal and decodable over
loops (Kohli et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026; Anonymous, 2026c), and a recurrent search circuit supports
depth generalization (Cheng and Zhang, 2026).
Wu et al. (2026) swap attention patterns (and, in an appendix, queries) between runs at the
same loop in an Ouro-2.6B model fine-tuned on letter transitivity, with one cross-loop control from
loop 2 into loop 4. In the released Ouro-2.6B, Same Head, Different Calls? (Anonymous, 2026a)
shows that a head-output patch can lose its effect one loop later, and What Does Another Loop
Buy? (Anonymous, 2026b) shows that freezing a loop’s attention pattern at its previous value
hurts GSM8K. None of these moves a later loop’s query into an earlier loop, compares
query, key and value freezes at fixed coordinates, or tests two-hop rescue. Methods that compress or share
the KV cache across
loops (O’Neill and Reid, 2026; Yang and Liu, 2026; Kim et al., 2026a; Monea et al., 2026; Conchello Vendrell et al., 2026; Ladhak et al., 2026; Mishra et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026)
or layers (Wu et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026) mostly train or adapt the model for sharing. In Huginn,
queries and keys approach their final geometry before values do (Li et al., 2026), and keys and
attention routing settle early in looped models (O’Neill and Reid, 2026; Wan and Chen, 2026); in Ouro, the query
still carries a needed update.
10 Limitations
We do not show where the composition is computed: loop 1 binds each person to a box (97.5% and 99.5% on
person→\tobox) yet fails on 42% and 35% of two-hop questions. Both models come from the Ouro family,
and Ouro-2.6B was grown from the 24-layer model; we make no claim about looped models in general. Our
dense controls are only two models, with depth offsets of at most 16 layers against 48 in Ouro-1.4B, so
we show neither that looping causes the rescue nor that dense transformers lack it. The mechanism results
come from synthetic scenes, and the freezes, swaps and converse test were not rerun on other templates.
On natural questions, the handoff is smaller in Ouro-2.6B (the replication covers 2WikiMultiHopQA only),
absent in Ouro-1.4B, and not shown to redirect retrieval. For the person→\tobox values, the matched
control cannot separate composition from the fact that only the two-hop question reads that line, and in
Ouro-2.6B the bridge-given question is near ceiling. Patched and frozen states are hybrids that the model
never produces natively; our controls bound generic damage but not the distance of these states from
natural ones. Query transplantation supplies late-loop information separately at every layer and head;
it does not show that a single query vector suffices, or that the recipient independently recomputes the
bridge. The crossed swap also leaves positional routing incompletely separated from entity identity.
Rescue is measured on questions selected for a loop-1 error and a loop-3 success; it does not establish
the net benefit of patching all questions. In Ouro-1.4B, the rescue moves items just across the decision boundary of a four-candidate
ranking, and loop 1 generates no answer, with or without the patch.
11 Conclusion
On synthetic two-hop questions, transplanting the layer-indexed collection of Ouro’s late-loop queries
at the answer position rescues most selected early-loop errors. Crossed swaps favor retrieval through
the donor’s bridge over answer copying, while leaving a substantial positional component in Ouro-2.6B.
Query freezes also damage two-hop questions more than matched questions that supply the bridge.
On natural questions, a smaller rescue replicates in Ouro-2.6B, but bridge redirection is not established.
These results identify transferable queries that support synthetic two-hop retrieval. Where the bridge
is computed, how memory updates contribute, and whether a single-state handoff can suffice remain open.
Impact statement
This paper analyzes how a pretrained looped language model answers two-hop questions. A better
understanding of where answers are read out may help to diagnose reasoning failures in such models. We
do not see societal consequences specific to this work that need to be highlighted here.

Appendix A Prompt formats
Synthetic two-hop.
Raw text, without a chat template. A two-hop prompt, verbatim:
Use only the facts in this scenario.
Box B contains red.
Box D contains blue.
Box C contains yellow.
Box A contains green.
Bob has box D.
Carol has box C.
Alice has box B.
David has box A.
Question: What color is in Bob’s box?
Answer:
The two one-hop prompts are identical except for the question: they end instead with “Question: Which
box does person have?” followed by “Answer: box” (candidates “ A”–“ D”), or with
“Question: What color is in box b?” followed by “Answer:”. Each question is a separate prompt,
and every candidate is a single token scored at the next position.
Natural QA.
The prompt, with passage text abbreviated; candidate answers are scored after the
final newline:
<|im_start|>system
You are a helpful assistant.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
Use only the following passages to answer the question. Give only the short answer.
Passages:
Title: <gold passage 1 title>
<gold passage 1 text>
Title: <gold passage 2 title>
<gold passage 2 text>
Question: <question><|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
The position qq is the last prompt token: the newline after the final <|im_start|>assistant. The
three distractors are gold answers of other items from the same dataset with the same answer type.
Appendix B Query transplantation procedure
The primary rescue uses a native donor run and an intervened recipient run of the same prompt. Crossed
swaps use different prompts. The mathematical intervention proceeds as follows:
1. 
Run the donor without intervention through loop ss and retain its post-RoPE queries
{Qℓ​h,q(s)}ℓ,h\{Q^{(s)}_{\ell h,q}\}_{\ell,h} at the final prompt position.
2. 
Run the recipient to loop rr. At each layer ℓ\ell, replace the post-RoPE query at qq in
every head hh with the donor query at the matching (ℓ,h)(\ell,h) coordinate.
3. 
Compute attention and the remainder of the layer with this replacement, then continue to the
next layer, where its corresponding donor queries are supplied. No key, value or residual-stream
activation is directly replaced in the query-rescue arm.
4. 
Read the recipient’s output at the loop-rr exit. For rescue, score only targets that were
wrong at native loop rr and right at native loop ss, using the same targets for the
same-example and shuffled-donor arms.
For the primary setting, r=1r=1 and s=3s=3. Causal attention ensures that positions before qq are
unaffected by these final-position query patches, so their keys and values remain native to the
recipient loop. At qq, however, the residual stream and later-layer keys and values can respond to
previous layers’ patches. The resulting forward pass is therefore a hybrid computation supplied with
late-loop queries throughout the stack, rather than a native early-loop computation initialized by
one late-loop vector. The cached donor information already required the donor’s later-loop computation;
the rescue experiment is a mechanistic intervention, not a demonstrated reduction in inference cost.
