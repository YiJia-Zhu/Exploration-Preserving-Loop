In conventional KD, soft labels preserve similarities that hard labels discard (Hinton et al., 2015).
For OPD, however, it remains unclear what in the
teacher’s next-token distributions contributes to better reasoning and where that contribution appears
along the student’s trajectory.
Prior work suggests that distillation quality depends on both what the teacher provides and what
the student can learn (Li et al., 2022). Therefore,
OPD training outcomes alone do not distinguish
the teacher’s available advantage from the student’s
ability to acquire it. We first examine the teacher’s
contribution through interventions that hold the student’s parameters fixed, then analyze how OPD
checkpoints change the disagreement.
In this paper, we focus on forks, positions at
which the student and teacher diverge in their nexttoken preferences. We measure the teacher’s advantage over the student through single and repeated
token flips. A single flip replaces the student’s token at one fork and lets the student continue. Dualtrack decoding repeats this one-token intervention
at selected forks as the trajectory develops.
We use these interventions to ask how much
accuracy teacher corrections recover and whether
selecting particular forks improves that recovery.
Checkpoint analysis then asks which preferences
OPD acquires and whether learning them produces
better answers. Our contributions are the following.

On-policy distillation (OPD) is increasingly
studied as a cheaper alternative to reinforcement learning, yet it remains a form of knowledge distillation (KD) that relies on teacher
supervision. We revisit OPD from the KD
perspective by asking where the teacher’s advantage lies along student-generated trajectories. Controlled token flips at teacher–student
disagreements directly measure the accuracy
gained from teacher corrections. Single-token
flips yield a modest average gain on selected
failed trajectories, whereas repeated flips raise
accuracy from 28.4% to 74.5%, close to the
teacher’s 76.9%. Gains continue to accumulate
with more flips, but mass- and KL-based selection outperform random flips by 15–18 percentage points at counts of similar magnitude. Selective intervention remains useful after OPD,
but greater argmax agreement does not consistently improve accuracy across model pairs.
We advocate training schedules that learn more
valuable teacher corrections while maintaining distributional stability. Controlled flips and
fork-resolution analysis evaluate OPD beyond
accuracy by measuring which teacher preferences the student acquires and what further correction can recover.
