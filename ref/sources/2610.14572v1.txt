Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models, but remains computationally expensive and sample inefficient. On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level supervision from a stronger teacher, yet standard OPD distills every token regardless of whether the teacher’s preference actually improves task reward. Existing token-selection methods rely on proxies such as uncertainty or teacher–student divergence, which need not identify reward-improving supervision. We introduce Reward-Aligned Distillation (RAD), a bilevel framework that weights distillation signals based on their predicted effect on the student’s post-update reward. RAD differentiates through a temporary optimizer step to score each token’s supervision, then updates the token weights by projected ascent from the initial token weights. The resulting weights can strengthen,
suppress, or reverse individual distillation-gradient contributions. At a matched training-response budget, RAD improves mean accuracy on six competition-math benchmarks over the strongest baselines by 6.5 percentage points for Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and 2.5 points for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. RAD-Simple, a simplified variant, achieves comparable math accuracy at the same training-response budget. On LiveCodeBench-v6, RAD reaches 54.69% and 58.67%
pass@1 with Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and OLMo3-7B-Instruct, respectively,
exceeding the strongest baselines by 8.7 and 6.4 points. These results support reward-guided weighting of teacher supervision across model families and tasks.

1 Introduction
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has been shown effective for improving
mathematical reasoning (Luong and Lockhart, 2025) and code generation (Jaech et al., 2024; Ekbote et al., 2025) abilities,
but its supervision is sparse: a long response often
receives only a single sequence-level reward
(Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). On-policy distillation offers a
complementary signal by evaluating student-generated responses under a
strong teacher and supplying dense token-level guidance
(Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). This approach is
appealing because the student continues to explore on policy while learning
from the teacher at every generated token.
Standard OPD, however, implicitly treats the teacher as the target policy and
every teacher preference as worth imitating. This would be justified if the
teacher were optimal for the target reward, but a stronger teacher need not be
reward-optimal. Teacher–student differences may reflect style, formatting,
verbosity, or reasoning choices that do not improve task reward (Armandpour et al., 2026). Consequently,
reducing teacher–student divergence does not guarantee reward improvement:
uniform distillation can spend capacity matching reward-irrelevant behavior or
move the student away from reward-improving solutions (Xu et al., 2026; Akhondzadeh et al., 2026).
The challenge is therefore to determine which teacher signals will benefit the student’s task performance. Token-selection methods (Xu et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026) use heuristic criteria such as uncertainty, teacher–student disagreement, and reasoning structure to selectively apply teacher supervision. These criteria identify potentially useful tokens, but do not directly assess whether the corresponding distillation update improves the student policy’s task reward. We therefore seek to choose each token’s distillation weight based on its predicted effect on reward: retain teacher signals that help the student solve the task, and suppress or reverse those that hinder it.
To this end, we introduce reward-aligned distillation (RAD), which weights teacher supervision by its predicted effect on task reward. Token weights affect reward indirectly, by controlling how the student learns from teacher supervision. RAD captures this dependency with a bilevel objective: the inner problem trains the student with weighted distillation, while the outer problem selects weights to maximize the trained student’s reward (Equation 2). We approximate the inner optimization with one temporary optimizer step using token weights initialized to a tunable value. Differentiating the temporary student’s reward with respect to these weights yields look-ahead scores that guide a projected ascent update. The resulting signed weights can strengthen, suppress, or reverse individual distillation-gradient contributions.
Main Contributions.
• 
We introduce Reward-Aligned Distillation (RAD), a bilevel framework that assigns
bounded, signed token weights according to their predicted effect on task reward (Section 4.2).
A one-step approximation yields optimizer-aware scores computed jointly through a single Jacobian–vector product (Section 4.3).
We also introduce RAD-Simple, which simplifies token scoring by removing the lookahead update and reusing distillation rollouts (Section 4.5).
• 
At matched training-response budgets, RAD and RAD-Simple improve mean accuracy across six math benchmarks over the
strongest baselines by up to 6.48 and 3.55 percentage points for Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, respectively.
On LiveCodeBench-v6, the corresponding pass@1 gains for RAD are 8.7 and 6.4 points for Qwen3-1.7B and OLMo3-7B-Instruct (Section 5).
• 
We demonstrate compatibility with an alternative distillation signal by applying RAD to OPD2.
This improves four-benchmark mean math accuracy by 2.66 and 6.51 percentage points for the 1.7B and 4B Qwen students, respectively (Section 6.3).
2 Related Work
On-policy distillation for LLMs. On-policy distillation queries a teacher on student-generated responses, reducing the training–inference mismatch of offline distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Related self-distillation methods obtain teacher signals from the student itself using privileged information such as verified solutions or environment feedback (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026).
Unifying distillation and RLVR. KDRL (Xu et al., 2025) & RLAD (Zhang et al., 2026) combine verifiable rewards with teacher guidance, while RG-OPD (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026) uses verifier outcomes and teacher likelihoods to gate trajectory-level distillation. ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026) scales implicit teacher-derived rewards relative to KL regularization.
Token-selective distillation. TIP (Xu et al., 2026) selects tokens using uncertainty and teacher–student divergence, while DEAR (Xiao et al., 2026) identifies decision tokens and supporting evidence. Reopold (Ko et al., 2026) combines teacher-derived reward clipping with entropy-guided token sampling.
Armandpour et al. (2026) develop an offline diagnostic of
reward–distillation alignment, estimating token-level QQ-values
by repeatedly sampling continuations after selected next-token choices,
which is computationally expensive. They measure
cosine similarity between local logit gradients and study oracle
filtering of positively aligned tokens.
RAD formulates token weighting as a bilevel optimization problem.
A one-step approximation yields the sensitivity of post-update reward
to each token’s weight, expressed as parameter-space alignment between
its distillation gradient and an optimizer-adjusted reward direction.
We estimate the reward gradient from minibatch rollouts, avoiding
explicit branchwise QQ-value estimation. Both the sign and magnitude
of the resulting scores guide projected ascent on bounded, signed
weights, which strengthen, suppress, or reverse distillation
contributions during training.
We compare against representative baselines from both reward-aware and token-selective distillation literature.
Extended related work appears in Appendix B.

3.1 Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a policy to
maximize rewards from verifiable outcomes, such as correct answers or passing
code tests. Given a reward function R⁡(𝒙,𝒚)R(\bm{x},\bm{y}), the expected-reward objective is defined as:
JR(𝜽)=𝔼𝒚∼π𝜽(⋅∣𝒙)[R(𝒙,𝒚)].J_{R}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x})}\left[R(\bm{x},\bm{y})\right].
Several policy-gradient surrogates can be used to optimize this objective in
RLVR, including proximal policy optimization (PPO)
(Schulman et al., 2017) and group relative policy optimization (GRPO)
(Shao et al., 2024). In this work, we use GRPO’s group-relative advantages
to estimate the reward gradient. For each prompt, we sample GG responses
{𝒚i}i=1G\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G} from the student and normalize their rewards within
the group:
AiR=R⁡(𝒙,𝒚i)−μRσR,μR=1G​∑j=1GR⁡(𝒙,𝒚j),A_{i}^{R}=\frac{R(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{i})-\mu_{R}}{\sigma_{R}},\mu_{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j}),
where σR\sigma_{R} is the group reward standard deviation. Every token in 𝒚i\bm{y}_{i} receives
the same response-level advantage AiRA_{i}^{R}.
3.2 On-Policy Distillation
On-policy distillation (OPD) samples responses from the student and evaluates
the teacher on the same student-generated states
(Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). For a fixed teacher
πT\pi_{\mathrm{T}}, the reverse-KL objective is defined as:
LD(𝜽)=𝔼𝒚∼π𝜽(⋅∣𝒙)[∑t=1Tlogπ𝜽​(yt∣st)πT​(yt∣st)].L_{D}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\bm{x})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{T}}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})}\right].
Unlike GRPO’s sequence-level reward, OPD provides dense supervision at every
generated token. Standard OPD, however, weights all token-level teacher
signals equally. Moreover, minimizing LDL_{D} alone provides no monotonic
improvement guarantee for JRJ_{R}.

Reward-Aligned Distillation (RAD) selects token-level distillation weights according to their predicted effect on task reward. We introduce the weighted objective (§4.1), then develop a bilevel formulation and its one-step approximation (§4.2). We extend the resulting scores to general optimizers and compute them efficiently using Jacobian–vector products (§4.3). Algorithm 1 summarizes our training recipe. Finally, we present RAD-Simple, a simplified variant using pre-update reward gradients and shared distillation rollouts (§4.5).
4.1 Token-Weighted Distillation

Appendix A.3 compares these initialization choices. Experiments indicate that c=0c=0 learns slower than c=1c=1 but can achieve better final performance. Gradually expanding the negative range from [0,1]\,[0,1]\, to [−1,1]\,[-1,1]\, for c=0c=0 can help balance learning speed and performance.
4.4 RAD Training Algorithm
Distillation rollouts are used to update the student, whereas meta rollouts
are used to estimate the reward gradient for token weighting. Both prompt
batches are sampled from the same training dataset.
RAD’s bilevel formulation makes token weighting depend on the student’s
post-update reward, introducing additional computation before each weighted
distillation update. At each step, we take a temporary AdamW step with
initial token weights c​𝟏c\mathbf{1}, generate separate meta rollouts,
and estimate the reward gradient using GRPO objective.
We compute the optimizer-aware token scores via a JVP
(Equation 7), update the weights with
Equation 5, and train the original student with the
updated weights. The meta batch is only used to learn the token weights; the committed
student update uses the distillation rollouts. Algorithm 1
summarizes the procedure of RAD, and Appendix A.2
gives the batch objective and implementation details.
RAD can in principle combine any differentiable token-level OPD objective
with any RLVR gradient estimator. Section 6.3
demonstrates compatibility with the OPD2 (Heo et al., 2026) distillation signal.
4.5 RAD-Simple: A Simplified Variant of RAD
The bilevel formulation adds a temporary
optimizer update, meta rollouts, a reward-gradient computation, and a JVP
to standard OPD. To simplify token scoring,
RAD-Simple estimates the pre-update reward gradient
by reusing the distillation rollouts.
Replacing the post-update reward gradient in Equation 4 by
the pre-update reward gradient gives
It​(𝒚)=−⟨∇𝜽JR​(𝜽),∇𝜽ℓD,t​(𝜽,𝒚)⟩.I_{t}(\bm{y})=-\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}J_{R}(\bm{\theta}),\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{D,t}(\bm{\theta};\bm{y})\right\rangle.
We omit the common factor η\eta, which can be absorbed into
the weight learning rate γ\gamma, and use the same projected
weight update in Equation 5.
RAD-Simple also omits the
optimizer Jacobian, while the student update still uses AdamW.
Reusing the distillation rollouts avoids the temporary student update and
separate meta rollout generation, halving the number of training responses per step.
The only additional computation compared with OPD are
reward-gradient estimation and a JVP. RAD-Simple
ignores changes in the reward gradient after distillation and the
optimizer’s effect on token scores. Table 1 compares
RAD-Simple with RAD at a matched training-response budget.

5 Experiments
We describe the experimental setup and evaluation protocol, then present math and coding results. Implementation details, including inference hyperparameters, appear in Appendix A.
5.1 Experimental Settings
We evaluate RAD on mathematical reasoning and code generation, and additionally evaluate RAD-Simple on mathematical reasoning.
Models. For mathematical reasoning, we use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 as the teacher and Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as students (Yang et al., 2025). For code generation, we evaluate Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) with the same teacher, and OLMo3-7B-Instruct with OLMo3.1-32B-Instruct as its teacher.
Training protocol. We train with verl (Sheng et al., 2025) using 64 prompts and eight responses per prompt. Within each domain and student setting, all methods share the training data, student initialization, rollout group size, sampling configuration, and optimizer settings. RAD additionally samples an equally sized, separate meta batch from the temporary student and updates token weights using exact JVPs. We compare 100 RAD updates with 200 baseline updates, matching the generated training responses, including RAD’s meta rollouts. At this budget, RAD uses 17–18% more training time than OPD and RLAD on Qwen3-1.7B math, but less than TIP and ExOPD. On OLMo3-7B coding, RAD uses 18–39% less training time than the reported baselines (Table 11). RAD uses the initial token weight c=0c=0 in the main math and coding comparisons, with an additional cosine schedule of negative range for coding. Shared and method-specific settings appear in Appendix A.1.
Baselines. We compare RAD and RAD-Simple with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024), TIP (Xu et al., 2026), RLAD (Zhang et al., 2026), and ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026). Given the higher training cost of the OLMo3-7B setting (Table 11), we retain GRPO and OPD as reference methods, with TIP and RLAD representing strongest token-selective and reward-aware distillation baselines, respectively. Baseline-specific hyperparameters follow the respective papers.
Mathematical reasoning. Following ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026), we train on 57,046 problems from DeepMath-103K (He et al., 2025) with difficulty ratings of at least 6. We evaluate on AIME24 (AI-MO, 2024), AIME25 (OpenCompass, 2025), AIME26, HMMT2025-Feb, HMMT2025-Nov (Balunovic et al., 2025), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). We sample 16 responses per problem at temperature 1.0 and top-pp 1.0, with a 32,768-token generation limit. Responses are graded with Math-Verify 11
 1
 https://github.com/huggingface/Math-Verify, and pass@1 is estimated as the fraction of sampled responses that are correct. The overall score is the unweighted mean across the six benchmarks.
Code generation. We train on the Eurus code split (Cui et al., 2025), assigning reward 1 when all tests pass and 0 otherwise. We evaluate on all 1,055 LiveCodeBench-v6 tasks (Jain et al., 2025a). Following ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026), we sample four responses per task at temperature 1.0 and top-pp 1.0, with a 32,768-token generation limit. Dataset statistics appear in Table 5.

RAD’s gain in this setting supports using post-update reward to guide the contribution of each teacher signal even when the initial teacher–student gap is small.

In this section, we examine which components drive RAD’s performance, how token reweighting
affects gradient alignment, and whether RAD extends to other distillation signals.
6.1 RQ1: Which components contribute to RAD’s performance?
We ablate RAD using Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) with c=0c=0 after 100 updates on AIME24/25 and HMMT2025-Feb/Nov (Table 2). Reversing scores or shuffling weights reduces accuracy from 32.71% to 0.36% and 6.35%, respectively, supporting token-specific alignment. Restricting weights to [0,1][0,1] yields 25.00%, while gradually introducing negative weights achieves 31.20%. Replacing AdamW lookahead with SGD yields 16.88%. These comparisons support signed weights and optimizer-aware scoring in this setting.
Evaluating the reward gradient before the temporary update achieves 34.32%, comparable to RAD.
However, it achieves 47.19% compared with 55.26% of RAD for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507,
showing the benefit depends on the student setting.
Reusing distillation rollouts further reduces accuracy to 18.54%, but also halves the generated responses per update. We next examine how token reweighting changes gradient alignment in RAD and RAD-Simple.

7 Conclusion and Future Work
RAD uses optimizer-aware lookahead scores and projected ascent to
align token-level distillation with reward. It improves math and coding
accuracy at a matched training-response budget. Results with
OLMo3-7B-Instruct extend the evidence to a different model family and a
larger student, while OPD2 results support compatibility with other
distillation signals.
RAD-Simple reuses distillation rollouts and removes the temporary update;
it improves over the strongest math baselines for both Qwen students at the same
training-response budget, demonstrating the effectiveness of simpler
token scoring in these settings.
Limitations and future work. RAD requires a temporary student update, fresh outer rollouts, and an exact JVP, increasing its per-step cost relative to standard OPD. RAD-Simple removes the temporary update and fresh outer rollouts but still requires reward-gradient estimation and a JVP. Further reducing these costs and understanding when optimizer-aware lookahead is beneficial remain important directions.
Future work should explore longer training horizons, and learned or non-verifiable rewards.

Appendix B Extended Related Work
On-policy distillation for LLMs. Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a larger teacher to a smaller student (Buciluǎ et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015). For LLMs, teacher supervision can take the form of token-level probability distributions or generated text (Kim and Rush, 2016; Sanh et al., 2020). Off-policy distillation trains on fixed sequences, often generated by the teacher (Hsieh et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). On-policy distillation instead queries the teacher on student-generated responses, exposing the student to supervision along its own trajectories and reducing the mismatch between training and inference (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Self-distillation paradigm obtains supervision from the student itself, with privileged information such as verified solutions or environment feedback providing a stronger teacher context (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026).
Unifying distillation and RLVR. Several methods combine dense teacher supervision with verifiable task rewards. KDRL (Xu et al., 2025) jointly optimizes a GRPO reward objective and reverse-KL distillation, while RLAD (Zhang et al., 2026) incorporates teacher guidance into policy optimization through a likelihood ratio anchored to a mixture of the teacher and the previous student policy. RG-OPD (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026) uses agreement between verifier outcomes and teacher likelihoods to gate trajectory-level distillation. A related perspective connects OPD to KL-regularized reinforcement learning: Yang et al. (2026) introduce a flexible reference model and scales the implicit reward derived from teacher probabilities, yielding the reward-extrapolation variant ExOPD. Our proposed method, RAD, uses task reward to determine how individual token-level distillation contributions should enter the student update.
Token-selective distillation. TIP (Xu et al., 2026) and DEAR (Xiao et al., 2026) select teacher supervision using uncertainty, teacher–student divergence, and reasoning structure. Reopold (Ko et al., 2026) combines token log-ratio rewards with clipping and entropy-guided sampling. Armandpour et al. (2026) diagnose token-level alignment between distillation and reward-improving directions in logit space and study oracle filtering of positively aligned tokens. RAD operationalizes reward-guided weighting during training: it differentiates a reward surrogate through the student optimizer and shared parameters to assign bounded, signed token weights. These weights can also reverse teacher contributions; our positive-only ablation supports the benefit of this additional flexibility. We compare RAD with representative reward-aware and token-selective baselines.
Bilevel reweighting. Learning example weights from a held-out objective is a standard use of
bilevel optimization (Ren et al., 2018). Gradient similarity has also
been used to adapt auxiliary losses toward a primary objective
(Du et al., 2018). We apply this principle at token granularity in
on-policy language-model distillation. The one-step approximation avoids
solving the full inner problem or an implicit linear system
(Liu et al., 2022; Kwon et al., 2023).

Query blocks stay within sequence boundaries, so packed sequences cannot
attend to one another. Causal, padding, and sliding-window masks use the
same valid query–key pairs as the primal; grouped-query heads share their
key/value tensors without explicitly repeating them. Our training launchers
use 16-query blocks, with Triton autotuning key-block sizes of 16 or 32.
Probability factors and tangent accumulators remain in FP32; the products
with VV and V˙\dot{V} use tf32x3 tensor-core precision.
The kernel stores O˙\dot{O} in the primal output’s dtype and L˙\dot{L} in FP32.
It avoids storing full attention or attention-tangent matrices, while
full-attention arithmetic remains quadratic in sequence length. The rule
analytically differentiates the attention equations up to floating-point error.
H.1 Layer-Streamed JVP Execution
The olmo3_layer_streamed backend evaluates the embedding, decoder
layers, final normalization, and output projection in sequence. For each
parameter group, the FSDP executor gathers its parameters and the corresponding
components of 𝒑\boldsymbol{p}, propagates both activations and activation
tangents, and releases the gathered tensors after use. It can prefetch the
next group, so gathered parameter and direction storage is bounded by the
current and prefetched groups. Activation states are retained for a bounded
set of microbatches at a time, with microbatch grouping synchronized across
ranks to preserve the order of collective operations.
Within each OLMo decoder layer, the backend uses the same FlashAttention
primal and Triton attention tangent as flash_triton
(Algorithm 2), including the masks for full and sliding-window
attention. Forward-mode differentiation propagates the chain-rule JVP through
successive layers, reducing the memory needed for gathered parameters and
their direction while retaining the token-score computation in
Equation 7.
