When to Follow the Teacher:
Advantage-Based On-Policy Distillation
Mahdi Sabbaghi* 1 , George Pappas1 , Hamed Hassani1 , and Adel Javanmard2

arXiv:2610.31563v1 [cs.LG] 22 Oct 2026

1 University of Pennsylvania

2 University of Southern California

Abstract
Online Policy Distillation (OPD) often outperforms offline methods such as Supervised Fine-Tuning
(SFT) by training on trajectories sampled directly from the student. However, standard OPD trains the student
to follow the teacher at every state of the trajectory, assuming that teacher guidance is always beneficial. We
instead distinguish between what the teacher prefers and what actually helps the current student succeed.
We introduce Advantage-Based On-Policy Distillation (AOPD), which weights teacher actions by their
counterfactual advantage under the student. Teacher actions that improve the student’s expected outcome are
reinforced, while those that do not are downweighted.
Directly computing these advantages requires trying alternative teacher actions at each step and running
new rollouts from each. Instead, we show that the AOPD objective can be estimated using only standard
student rollouts and their terminal rewards. Our estimator uses teacher importance weights on the studentsampled actions to provide unbiased advantage-weighted supervision, without any additional rollouts or
reward evaluations. This allows AOPD to focus teacher supervision on actions that help the current student
while preserving the rollout efficiency of standard OPD. Experiments on math, coding, and Medqa show
gains over distillation and RL baselines in both external-teacher and self-distillation settings.

Introduction

Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a stronger teacher to a smaller student model by training
the student to imitate the teacher’s outputs. Offline distillation is a common approach that uses fixed teachergenerated trajectories. But this approach can lead to a mismatch between the training trajectories and the states
the student generates itself. On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses this mismatch by querying the teacher
along student-sampled trajectories, providing dense token-level supervision at the states the student actually
visits (Agarwal et al., 2024; Lu and Lab, 2025; Song and Zheng, 2026; Ross et al., 2011; Li et al., 2026a).
Although the trajectories now come from the student, the objective remains to match the teacher, without
distinguishing which teacher actions improve the student’s chance of success (Liu et al., 2026).
This limitation also applies to on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), which replaces the external teacher with
a privileged version of the student itself, such as conditioned on ground-truth solutions (Zhao et al., 2026;
Hübotter et al., 2026). Whether external or privileged, the teacher may choose an action from which it can
solve the problem, while the student cannot complete the remaining steps correctly. Conversely, distillation
discourages a valid student solution when it differs from the teacher’s preferred approach. Thus the question
is whether the teacher action at every state increases the student’s chance of success. An action should be
credited according to its impact on the student’s task success, not simply the teacher’s preference (Chen et al.,
2026; Saadi and Wang, 2026).
