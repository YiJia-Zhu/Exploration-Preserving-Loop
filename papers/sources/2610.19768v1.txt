Abstract
Looped transformers compute by applying one block repeatedly, so they can run
for more loops than they were trained with. Recurrent reasoners are known to
overthink: iterating past the trained depth can turn a correct answer into a
wrong one. Such a late error can reflect failed computation, loss of
retention, or a state that later computation cannot reuse. We separate these cases in in-context
Mealy-machine execution, where a simulator supplies intermediate targets and
exact counterfactuals for hidden-state interventions. Models trained to answer
at loop 32 are fully correct there but keep only 22% of these answers at
loop 64. Retention depends on what training asks for: supervising one randomly
chosen position per example, instead of always the last, keeps 99.8%, and
randomizing the number of training loops also helps; truncating gradients, as
in progressive training against overthinking, does not. The computation itself is
exact: position kk is computed at loop kk, and swapping its loop-kk hidden
vector for the one from another input redirects every later position to that
input’s exact continuation (237/237 pairs in each of ten models). Retention,
however, is not reuse. Even in models that keep nearly all answers, holding
each position kk fixed from loop kk drops accuracy to 4–6%, whereas
holding it from loop k+4k+4 is harmless. A released recurrent maze network
trained to avoid overthinking behaves the same way. Learning to compute is thus
not learning to retain, and avoiding overthinking does not make a state
reusable when its answer first appears.
1 Introduction
Looped transformers turn recurrent depth into a computation budget: one block
is applied repeatedly, so a model can run for more loops than it was trained
with (Dehghani et al., 2019; Giannou et al., 2023; Geiping et al., 2025).
Whether those extra loops help depends on what happens to an answer after it
has been computed.
Empirically, running past the trained depth can hurt. Recurrent networks
trained on small instances can solve larger ones by iterating longer
(Schwarzschild et al., 2021), but further iteration can also turn a correct
answer into a wrong one. Bansal et al. (2022) call this failure
overthinking and prevent it with input recall and progressive
training, which applies the loss at a random depth and blocks gradients to
the loops before a random starting point. Later work relates extra
computation to path independence and shows that training and stopping
objectives change the learned trajectory
(Anil et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026; Kuo et al., 2026; Popescu et al., 2026);
analysis of recurrent language models separates whether content is readable
from whether it is transported and can be steered (Wang and Reid, 2026).
Much of this evidence tracks accuracy or readouts across loops. An
overthinking curve, however, can mean three different things:
the answer was never computed, it was computed but not
retained, or the state that carries it cannot be reused by
later computation. Telling these apart requires knowing what a correct
alternative execution would be. We therefore ask: when a looped model
learns to compute an answer, what does it learn about retaining that answer
and reusing its internal state?
In-context execution of Mealy machines makes these distinctions measurable
(Figure 1). Each example gives a transition table and an
input sequence of length HH. A simulator supplies the correct state and
output at every position, including after any counterfactual change, so an
intervention can be scored against a unique correct continuation rather
than by whether the output changed. We apply one shared transformer block for
RR loops and train only on the readout after the last loop, the exit;
the main models use R=HR=H, one loop per input symbol, and compare objectives that differ only in which answers must be correct at
that exit: the last position (final-only), every position (all-prefix), or
one uniformly sampled position per example (sampled-prefix). Endpoint
accuracy requires both the state and the output at the last position to be
correct. In the models we analyze, position kk first becomes correct at or near
loop kk; we call the diagonal r=kr=k the frontier. Because the loop at
which each answer first appears is known, a state can be tested at exactly
that moment. We intervene on the recurrent
trajectory in three ways: replacing a hidden vector in a base run with
the corresponding vector of a source run on a different input prefix
(Geiger et al., 2024), resetting a position to its own earlier vector,
and freezing each position at a chosen loop. At the frontier, one
position’s hidden vector suffices to drive the rest of the execution, so
the same unit lets us compare what a state makes readable with what it lets
later computation do.
The three properties come apart. In the order of the paper, we find:
• 
Retention is set by what training requires
(Section 3). On shared length-32 test inputs,
final-only models are 100% correct at the trained exit but 22.3% at
loop 64 (Figure 2). all-prefix gives 94.3%, and
sampled-prefix gives 99.8% with the same single supervised position per
example as final-only. At a matched expected budget, drawing the exit as
R=H+UR=H+U with UU uniform on {0,…,8}\{0,\ldots,8\}, instead of fixing R=H+4R=H+4,
raises loop-64 accuracy from 30.0% to 85.6%. Truncated gradients do not
help: our adaptation of progressive training learns length 32 in one of
three seeds, whereas a matched random exit control with identical exits and full
gradients learns it in all three and keeps 97.9% at loop 64.
• 
Computation is exact, but its answers are transient
(Section 4). Replacing one hidden vector at the frontier
redirects every later frontier readout to the exact counterfactual execution
in all 237 pairs whose counterfactual differs, in each of ten final-only models (Figure 3). These answers fade soon after they are
computed, in swapped and unmodified runs alike.
• 
Answers become readable before their states become reusable
(Section 5). Freezing each position kk from the loop at
which its answer first becomes readable, as per-position halting would and
with no other run or label involved, reduces endpoint accuracy from 100% to
4.1% under sampled-prefix and to 6.3% under matched random exit, two objectives
that retain answers well (Figure 4). Freezing two to four
loops later restores it. A released recurrent maze network that never loses
a solved maze solves none of 32 mazes when each pixel is frozen at its
answer’s arrival, and all of them when frozen four iterations later.
These interventions separate the three properties; they do not identify a
microscopic cause of ordinary decline or give a general repair. The main
evidence comes from one synthetic task family and one architecture, with a
limited external check (Section 7).

Evaluation cohorts.

Readouts and scoring.
For one model and NN evaluation examples of length HH, endpoint accuracy
at readout loop rr is the fraction for which both predictions at the last
action are correct:
A(H,r)=1N∑i=1N[q^i,Hr=qi,H∧y^i,Hr=yi,H].A(H,r)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{q}_{i,H}^{r}=q_{i,H}\;\land\;\hat{y}_{i,H}^{r}=y_{i,H}\right].
(4)
Whole-trajectory exactness requires both predictions at all HH
positions of an example to be correct at the same loop rr, and reports the
fraction of examples satisfying this conjunction. The main endpoint and
trajectory evaluations use 512 traces per model and length: 16 machines
with 32 traces each. Scores are computed within each model and then averaged
across models with equal weight (Appendix A).

for the specified model, horizon, and evaluation set. This population

τ⁡(k)\tau(k) for this measured quantity and write s⁡(k)s(k) for a specified readout
or capture schedule. The matched-frontier audit in Section 4
uses the diagonal schedule s⁡(k)=ks(k)=k; a common-exit readout uses s⁡(k)=rs(k)=r for
all positions. Fixed freezing schedules and their agreement with the clean

A correct endpoint need not survive more loops.
Figure 2a,b evaluates the common cohort, holding each input
and checkpoint fixed across loops. final-only endpoint joint
accuracy falls from 100% at loop 32 to 60.81% at loop 33 and 22.33% at
loop 64. Whole-trajectory exactness is 0% at both loops 32 and 64.
These are losses on solved inputs of unchanged length. Original-cohort
results remain separate (Appendix B.2).
One supervised position per example is enough for strong retention.
At the same training exit R=HR=H, all-prefix raises loop-64 endpoint accuracy
to 94.27% on the common stream, but supplies more supervised positions.
sampled-prefix controls this difference: each example contributes one
uniformly chosen position’s state and output losses. All three models attain
100% endpoint and whole-trajectory accuracy at loop 32. At loops 64 and 96,
endpoint means are 99.80% and 96.61%, and whole-trajectory means are
96.03% and 74.09%. The corresponding all-prefix trajectory means are
74.28% and 67.45%.
Dense per-example supervision is therefore not necessary for the observed
retention gain. This three-seed result does not establish that sampling is
generally superior to all-prefix, nor remove the need for intermediate labels.
For both baselines trained at R=HR=H, the original readability grids give a
first-correct frontier τ⁡(k)=k\tau(k)=k (Appendix B.4);
Section 4 tests its causal role.
Varying the exit helps at matched expected loop budgets.
The original-cohort comparison in Figure 2c retains
final-only supervision and compares Slack-4, R=H+4R=H+4, with Jitter-8,
R=H+U​{0,…,8}R=H+U\{0,\ldots,8\}. Both have conditional mean H+4H+4; cumulative
batched block applications differ by only 0.062%. At H32/loop64, endpoint
means are 30.01% and 85.55%, with Jitter-8 higher in every pair; at
loop96 they are 26.79% and 79.79%. Both readouts exceed Jitter-8’s H32

Slack-4 gives 93.98% versus 87.27%, and some seeds do not fully learn the
training length range. Appendix B.3 reports all seeds and work
accounting.
Truncated gradients do not help retention.
Bansal et al. (2022) prevent overthinking with progressive training,
which changes two things at once: the loss is applied at a random depth, and
gradients do not reach the loops before a random starting point. We adapt it
to this architecture and separate the two with a matched control. At each
update, both progressive and matched random exit add to a full branch ending at
m=H+8m=H+8 a second branch with a random exit n+k≥Hn+k\geq H. progressive detaches this branch after its first nn loops; matched random exit back-propagates through all n+kn+k. Examples, exits, loss weights, and
updates are identical, so only the gradient path, and with it the backward
work, differs (Appendix B.1).
In this recipe truncation impedes learning. Two of three progressive models never exceed 60% H32 endpoint accuracy, so their low late scores are
not forgetting; the third reaches 100% at loops 40–48 and falls to 26.6%
by loop 96. All three matched random exit models reach 100% at loop 32 and keep
97.9% at loop 64 (Figure 2d). Like Jitter-8, this
random-exit objective keeps endpoint answers well past its training exits.
Its losses, like those of final-only, use only the last position, and it
keeps far fewer complete trajectories: 29.6% are exact at loop 64, against
96.0% under sampled-prefix. This is an FSM adaptation, not a reproduction
of the original architecture or a ranking of progressive methods.
Retention is a distinct, finite-window property.
Supervision support does not fully explain learning or retention, and H32
retention does not guarantee improved length extrapolation.
On the common H64/loop96 evaluation, sampled-prefix has 82.68% endpoint
accuracy versus 93.03% for matched random exit. The original length comparison also
has mixed outcomes (Appendix B.5).
4 Computation is exact, but its answers are transient
Section 3 found that each position’s answer first becomes
readable at loop kk, even in final-only models that get almost no complete
trajectory right at the trained exit. Readable does not mean used: a readout
can decode information that the computation ignores. We therefore test
whether the frontier state is what the rest of the execution runs on. If it
is, replacing it with another input’s state should make the run follow that
input’s execution exactly, and a simulator tells us what that execution is.
Swapping one state between two inputs.
We pair two inputs that share the machine, symbol assignment, and
transition-table presentation, and differ only in their actions up to
position cc; their states qcSq_{c}^{S} and qcBq_{c}^{B} at cc differ
(Figure 3a). Following interchange interventions
(Geiger et al., 2024), we copy the full 128-dimensional hidden vector of
the source run at position cc and loop rr into the base run,
hcr←hc,Sr,h_{c}^{r}\leftarrow h_{c,S}^{r},
(5)
leave every other base position unchanged, and let the trained model
continue. No symbolic state or answer enters the network. Because the
remaining actions are shared, a base run that now carries the source’s state
should continue exactly as the source run does. Running
Equation 1 from qcSq_{c}^{S} on these actions gives this
counterfactual target q~k\tilde{q}_{k} for every k>ck>c (Geiger et al., 2021).
A pair is suffix-exact under a readout schedule s⁡(k)s(k) when every
state and output after cc matches the target. We score only eligible
pairs, whose counterfactual and factual suffixes differ, so that a run
ignoring the swap cannot succeed (Appendix A).

One swapped state drives the rest of the execution.
In ten final-only models with H=32H=32, we swap at position 8 and loop 8,
the loop at which position 8’s answer first becomes correct in every model
(τ⁡(8)=8\tau(8)=8; Appendix A). Of 256 pairs, 237 are eligible. In every
model, all 237 follow the source exactly: every state and output at
positions 9 to 32, each read at loop kk (Figure 3c). One
vector thus steers 24 further steps of exact symbolic execution. Copying a
vector back into its own run changes no prediction, and patch scans place the
damaging site on the same frontier (Appendix A). The swap
shows that this state suffices to drive the computation; it does not show
that the state is a unique symbolic code.
The answers do not stay.
In both the swapped and the clean run, agreement is highest on the diagonal
and fades above it (Figure 3b,c): an answer is readable at
the loop that computes it, and answers computed early have faded by loop 32.
The near-zero whole-trajectory exactness of final-only models at the trained
exit (Section 3) is therefore not a failure to compute: the
intermediate answers are computed exactly and then not kept.

Halting treats a solved position as finished.
Section 3 showed that training can keep an answer readable
long after it is computed, and Section 4 that a single
frontier state drives the computation that follows. A position whose answer
has appeared and stays readable therefore looks finished, and adaptive
computation treats it that way. The Universal Transformer halts positions
individually and copies a halted position’s state unchanged to every later
step (Dehghani et al., 2019). Huginn stops iterating on a token once
successive steps give nearly the same output distribution, and later tokens
attend to its last computed key–value state (Geiping et al., 2025).
Bansal et al. (2022) describe their networks, trained to avoid
overthinking, as holding on to the representation of a solution once it is
reached. These methods differ in when a position counts as finished. We test
the earliest point, the loop at which its answer first becomes readable. Our
setting makes that loop known: position kk first becomes correct at loop
kk, and every position has an exact target.
Freezing applies this halting at the first readable loop.
For each position kk, we capture its hidden vector at loop kk and write it
back after every later block, as copying a halted state would. All other
positions update normally, frozen positions stay visible to attention, and no
other run or label is involved (Figure 4a). As a control,
readout caching stores each position’s loop-kk prediction outside
the model and leaves every hidden state untouched; the two differ only in
whether the answer or its state is kept. We call a state reusable if
freezing it in this way leaves the later positions correct. We freeze six
final-only, three all-prefix, and three larger final-only models (d=512d=512
and four layers per block, about 32 times the block parameters), and the
three sampled-prefix and three matched random exit models of
Section 3.
The answers are correct, but their states cannot yet be frozen.
In the twelve models of Figure 4b, caching keeps all 32
answers correct, so every answer is correct when it is captured. Freezing the
states instead leaves at most 2% of examples fully correct. Good retention
does not prevent this. sampled-prefix keeps 99.8% of its endpoint answers
at loop 64 (Section 3) and its cached answers are all
correct, yet freezing drops its endpoint accuracy from 100% to 4.1%.
all-prefix models, which keep every position readable at the exit, and
matched random exit models collapse in the same way (Appendix D).
The answers stay readable; their states cannot yet stop changing.
Frozen states fail as inputs to later computation.
The errors start just downstream of the frozen states
(Figure 4c). Positions 1 and 2 stay correct in every model,
and position 3 is the first to fail. At loop 3 it reads the loop-2 vectors
of positions 1 to 3, which differ from the clean run in one place only:
position 1 has missed its loop-2 update, which came after its answer was
already readable. That one missing update lowers the accuracy of position 3
to 68% in the final-only models, and errors then compound along the
sequence. Freezing only positions 1–8 leaves positions
9–32 free to update, yet in the final-only models the endpoint is still
correct on at most 11.9% of examples (Appendix D). A frozen
state still yields its own answer, as positions 1 and 2 show, but it no
longer works as input: later positions need the updates that upstream states
receive after their answers appear.
A state becomes reusable two to four loops after its answer.
Delaying the capture by dd loops shows when a state can be frozen
(Figure 4d). With a two-loop delay, sampled-prefix recovers
to 99.8% endpoint accuracy; matched random exit needs four loops to reach the same
level (Appendix D.3). An answer is readable at loop kk,
but the state that carries it becomes reusable only a few loops later, and
how many loops depends on the model.
The maze network trained against overthinking behaves the same way.
We apply the same test to the released maze network of
Bansal et al. (2022), the model described above as holding on to its
solution. Trained with 30 iterations, it solves all 32 test mazes of size
9×99\times 9 at every iteration from 25 to 120. Since mazes have no
per-position frontier, we freeze each pixel at its answer’s arrival, the last
iteration at which its clean prediction changes. No maze is then solved;
freezing two or four iterations later solves 28 and 32, and caching the
answers solves all 32 (Appendix D; one checkpoint, 32 mazes
per size). The network does hold on to its solution, but only a few
iterations after each answer arrives.
What freezing shows.
An answer readable at loop kk is thus not yet carried by a reusable state,
even in models that retain answers almost perfectly; the state becomes
reusable two to four loops later. Retention and reuse are different
properties. For per-position halting in such models, stopping a position when
its answer first becomes readable is too early: its state must keep updating
for a few loops, or only its answer should be kept. The experiments do not show
that information is lost, since cached answers stay correct, and they do not
identify a mechanism. The model has no positional or loop embeddings, so the
evolving state may also carry timing or order information that later
positions use to find their predecessors, and freezing would hold that
information as well. Our tests do not separate these possibilities; under
either, a state cannot be frozen when its answer first appears. A source
vector transplanted from another run shows the same dependence on timing
(Appendix C).
6 Related work
Extra iterations in recurrent reasoners.
Recurrent networks trained on small instances can solve larger ones by
iterating longer (Schwarzschild et al., 2021). Bansal et al. (2022)
show that excessive iteration can degrade answers (“overthinking”), and
prevent this with input recall and progressive training from detached
intermediate states. They also test recovery after feature-map interventions
and track feature convergence. We adapt their progressive loss in
Section 3 and freeze their released maze network in
Section 5. Anil et al. (2022) relate additional
computation to path independence: convergence to the same steady state
regardless of initialization. We do not claim novelty for overthinking,
variable exits, or mid-computation substitution. What is new is the
measurement: exact symbolic targets for every position, loop, and
counterfactual, which let us test a state at the loop where its answer
first appears.
These works assess stability through outputs, feature maps, or final
convergence; we ask whether a state can be held from the moment its clean
readout becomes correct. This is a position-level counterpart to path
independence, not a test of it: we do not vary initialization.
Recurrent computation and training requirements.
Universal and looped transformers connect shared parameters to adaptive
computation, programmable execution, and length generalization
(Dehghani et al., 2019; Giannou et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2025); recurrent-depth
language models extend this motivation to latent test-time compute
(Geiping et al., 2025). Zhang et al. (2026) study budget-dependent
frontiers, causal damage cones, and frontier-state transplants. We build on
these observations rather than claiming novelty for frontiers or transport.
A first-correct frontier differs from a convergence time that requires an
answer to remain correct.
Stochastic stopping can stabilize budget-dependent behavior without better
extrapolation (Kuo et al., 2026); adaptive-depth studies distinguish
trajectory formation from readout selection (Popescu et al., 2026).
Our comparisons instead change supervised positions at a fixed exit, or exit
variability at matched expected loops; sampling one position estimates the
all-position loss rather than introducing a new loss principle.
Readable and causally usable representations.
Wang and Reid (2026) distinguish readout, transport, and steering across
recurrent depth in Ouro and Huginn, and associate verbalizing injected content
with per-iteration supervision. Our all-prefix loss applies only at the
exit; we add paired training comparisons, exact symbolic targets, and local
downstream restoration that separates downstream state from execution phase.
State-tracking studies identify alternative transformer algorithms and effects
of intermediate objectives (Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025),
and interchange interventions test such algorithmic hypotheses
(Geiger et al., 2021; Geiger et al., 2024). Our full-vector intervention
establishes sufficiency in a specified machine context, not a unique minimal
encoding or complete algorithm identification.
7 Discussion and limitations
Computing an answer, retaining its readout, and reusing its representation
are different properties, and each needs its own test: correctness at one exit
does not test retention, and retained predictions do not test whether a hidden
state can be held or reused for continuation. Supervised positions, exit distributions, and
gradient propagation all change retention, and poor late accuracy can conceal
a coherent earlier counterfactual computation. Retained answers need not
reside in reusable states: freezing a position when its answer first becomes
readable disrupts later computation, including under sampled-position
training, and the state becomes reusable only a few loops later; a
transplanted source likewise needs the downstream position reset before it
takes effect. We have not tested downstream resets on the new training controls or
stochastic-budget models, so their retention gains do not establish the same
downstream mechanism.
These full-vector, post-block interventions do not identify a unique
encoding, microscopic cause of forgetting, or general repair; they can change
nonsymbolic features or restart computation, and downstream recovery concerns
one step at one site and age. The scope is one synthetic execution family and one principal
architecture, plus a limited external maze check. Original training
comparisons use six paired seeds; new controls use three. Re-evaluating
overlapping checkpoints is not independent model replication. Sampled
supervision still needs intermediate labels. Budget controls match expected
loops, and progressive controls match forward work but differ in backward
work; neither matches measured total FLOPs. Finite-window retention does
not establish hidden-state convergence or uniform length extrapolation.

Appendix A Experimental methods and scoring
A.1 Machine generation and input encoding
At each stage, the data generator samples the state count and input-alphabet
size independently and uniformly from the active lists in
Table A1; the output alphabet has two symbols. Generation
fixes an internal initial state S0S_{0} and first makes every state reachable.
In a random order of the other states, each is attached through a uniformly
sampled unused state–input cell of an already reachable state. Remaining
transition destinations are sampled uniformly from QQ, and each cell’s
output is sampled uniformly from Γ\Gamma. Candidates are resampled until
behaviorally minimal and covering both output symbols. These construction
and acceptance rules make the accepted transition-table entries dependent.
Each sampled machine contributes up to 32 action traces, all starting at
S0S_{0}, with actions drawn independently and uniformly from its input alphabet.
A training batch contains 256 examples of the same sampled horizon. The
online stream, including machine generation, action traces, and presentation
randomization, is reproducible from its generator state.
The initial state is one token. Every transition is represented by one
factorized token containing its source state, input symbol, destination state,
and output symbol. Action tokens follow the table and contain only their
input symbol. Token embeddings sum a kind embedding and the embeddings of the
present symbolic fields. The global namespaces contain 16 state IDs, 8 input
IDs, and 4 output IDs, although an individual machine uses only 4/6/8 states,
2/4 inputs, and 2 outputs. Each presentation assigns local symbols to randomly
selected namespace IDs and shuffles the transition rows. Training does not
force traces from one machine to share the same presentation; source–base pairs
explicitly share a presentation to control that variable.
The attention mask lets initial-state and table tokens attend within the
prefix, and action kk attend to the prefix and actions up to and including
kk. It blocks all access to future actions. Padded positions are masked and
zeroed through the computation. A causal action prefix therefore has the same
unmodified computation when followed by additional actions of the same
presented machine. This structural property helps explain why endpoint
training at shorter lengths constrains earlier positions in longer examples;
it does not prove a particular internal algorithm.
Training uses data seed 20260901. The original six-seed budget comparisons use
evaluation seed 20260909; the five-condition, three-seed comparison uses
20260923, with the old baselines re-evaluated on that same stream. Both use
four batches of 128 examples per model and length: 16 sampled machines with
32 traces each, not 512 independent machines. The new six-model freeze
follow-up uses stream 20260924. Actual generator seeds add 1009​H1009H to the
specified stream. The original training logs use evaluation seed 20260902.
These are separate evaluation streams. We do not
combine their metrics as if they came from one paired sample set. Randomized
presentations and independently drawn evaluation streams do not guarantee
that every machine graph is structurally absent from training.

A.6 Controls and statistical interpretation
True self-replacement copies the base run’s own vector at the same site and
time, and must preserve predictions exactly. Same-symbolic-state sources come
from another history and are a substantive intervention, not an identity
control. A positive source score is interpreted alongside the clean base run’s
agreement with its own target and with the source target. Chance coincidence
with a source target is not causal transfer. A clean factual score is a
reference level, not a formal upper bound on intervention performance.
Means first aggregate the specified pair/sample population within each model,
then average models with equal weight. Model seeds are independent parameter
initializations, whereas reused machine/pair samples are paired observations.
We preserve per-seed outcomes rather than converting task pairs into a larger
number of independent training runs. All intervals and differences in the
main text are descriptive; no null-hypothesis significance claim is based on
treating those repeated pairs as independent models.

H≤n+k≤mH\leq n+k\leq m, so neither accepted nn nor the exit is uniform.
Both conditions optimize 12​Lm+12​Ln+k\tfrac{1}{2}L_{m}+\tfrac{1}{2}L_{n+k} using endpoint
labels. The mm-loop branch has full gradients. For the random-exit branch,
progressive runs nn loops without a gradient graph, detaches the hidden
state, and differentiates through the remaining kk loops. Input embeddings
are recomputed with gradients for injection into this continuation and
absolute loop indices are preserved. The n=0n=0 case starts normally with
gradients. matched random exit uses the same examples, draws, exits, and loss
weights, but differentiates through all n+kn+k loops. At fixed weights and
zero dropout, the two losses have equal values but different gradients.
This is an FSM adaptation, not a reproduction of the original convolutional
architecture, concatenated recall, or exit support; it is also distinct
from the uniform Jitter-8 contract below.

Appendix C Local downstream recovery and source-phase controls
The phase-crossing experiment changes the base run’s entire execution
phase. Here we keep the late background fixed and test whether changing one
local downstream vector can restore the response to a fresh source. The
protocol was fixed before inspecting this follow-up’s outputs; it did not
retrospectively preregister the preceding experiments.

Appendix D Frontier freezing
D.1 Original cohort and protocol
The original freezing cohort uses the frozen checkpoints of Section 5 on a new
evaluation stream (20260923), 512 inputs per model and horizon, with the
same inputs for every arm of a given model. The schedule is fixed before
scoring: models trained at R=HR=H use s⁡(k)=ks(k)=k. Frontier freezing captures
position kk’s post-block hidden vector at loop s⁡(k)s(k) on the arm’s own
ongoing trajectory and restores it after every later block. One loop
later captures at s⁡(k)+1s(k)+1. Positions 1–8 freezes only those
positions, so every later position must still compute. Readout cache
runs the clean forward pass and delivers each position’s prediction saved at
s⁡(k)s(k), without modifying any hidden state. No vector comes from a
separate run, no label enters an intervention, and table and presentation
tokens are never frozen. Frozen positions remain available to attention.
All layers of a block still execute; freezing acts at recurrent block
boundaries, not on individual layers or key–value caches. The protocol was
written before the results were inspected but is exploratory, not an
independent confirmation.

D.2 Additional observations and limits
Table A18 reports every evaluated model. Under frontier
freezing, positions 1 and 2 remain correct and accuracy at each position’s
scheduled capture loop falls from position 3 onward in all three groups. Capturing one
loop later is not uniformly harmless. It lowers the endpoint to 74–77% in
three of the twelve models at H=32H=32. On the separate H=64H=64 length
evaluation at readout r=H=64r=H=64, it lowers the endpoint
relative to clean in several final-only models (seed 12: 44.1% versus
99.2%; seed 14: 46.3% versus 95.7%). One-loop-later freezing is therefore
a timing contrast, not a safe schedule. double models, whose observed clean frontier is
τ⁡(k)=2​k−1\tau(k)=2k-1, collapse under both schedules; their clean endpoint at r=Hr=H
is already low because they need about 2​H2H loops, so they are not
interpreted. The readout cache preserves the captured predictions by
construction and is correct on every original H32 example. It is the
baseline any freezing schedule would have to beat; we propose no such
schedule.

Results and limits.
Immediate freezing reduces mean endpoint accuracy to 4.10% for
sampled-prefix and 6.25% for matched random exit, with zero
whole-trajectory exactness in every model. Thus the failure to hold
position states extends across these training objectives, including three
models with fully correct clean scheduled reads. Delaying capture helps,
but the required delay depends on the model and training condition.
At delay 2, sampled-prefix reaches 99.80% mean endpoint accuracy and
99.54% scheduled trajectory exactness. matched random exit reaches only
87.24% endpoint accuracy at delay 2, and needs delay 4 to reach 99.80%
endpoint accuracy and 99.74% scheduled trajectory exactness. All fixed
delays and all seeds are reported; one loop is not a generally safe
schedule. Prefix-only freezing also damages the never-frozen endpoint
and suffix, so the collapse is not limited to scoring held positions.
The two objectives differ in losses and training work, precluding a unique
attribution of their delay difference to one training factor. These
results concern holding a state on its own evolving trajectory; they do
not demonstrate arbitrary vector interchangeability, convergence, or a
particular microscopic circuit.

D.4 External maze network
We evaluated the released dt_net_recall_2d checkpoint (width 128,
trained on 9×99\times 9 mazes for 30 iterations with the progressive loss of
Bansal et al., 2022) without modification, on 32 pre-selected mazes
per size for iterations 1–120. The recurrence was replayed by an external
loop that reproduces the released forward pass exactly; a never-triggering
hook reproduced the clean arm, and every arm matched clean before its first
capture. Because there is no per-position frontier, a pixel’s capture time
is its answer arrival: the last iteration at which its clean
prediction changes (mean 6.2 at 9×99\times 9, 19.7 at 33×3333\times 33).

The clean network solves every 9×99\times 9 maze from iteration 25 through
120, so it does not forget answers, yet freezing at answer arrival solves
none. The limits are substantial. There is one checkpoint, no training
seeds, and 32 mazes per size. Architecture, task, and objective differ from

pixel’s state does not pin its prediction (2.9% of frozen pixels drift at
capture +0+0, 0.3% at +4+4, 9×99\times 9); its frozen-readout quantities are
not comparable with those of our per-position readout. The
33×3333\times 33 size lies outside the training distribution and its clean
accuracy is still rising at iteration 120, so those comparisons are
descriptive.

Results and limits.
Pooled over the d=128d=128 models, the position-31, position-32, and combined resets
recover 585, 143, and 771 of 771 members of EE. Loop-33 accuracy on all
inputs is 89.5%, 35.2%, and 100%, against 74.9% clean. For d=512d=512 the
counts are 41, 59, and 79 of 79, with all-input accuracy of 95.3%, 79.1%,
and 100% against 94.9% clean. Single resets are not ranked: the position-31
reset leads in d=512d=512 seed 9, the position-32 reset in seeds 10 and 11, and a position-32
reset alone breaks between 130 and 350 correct inputs in every d=128d=128
model. The factorial interaction varies in sign across seeds, so we do not
claim superadditivity.
The combined reset leaves positions 1–30 one loop ahead but otherwise
approximately replays loop 32 at the endpoint, which was correct. Its
success therefore localizes the first failure to the last two positions; it
does not show that a restored state persists. Without a second
intervention, d=128d=128 all-input accuracy at loop 34 is 63.7% after the
combined reset versus 65.5% clean, whereas for d=512d=512 it is 93.4% versus
76.8%. We report the relapse as a d=128d=128 observation only. The experiment
identifies joint sufficiency at one horizon and loop; it does not identify a
circuit, show that either reset is necessary, or establish a repair method.
